I wrote `steal.py`, ran it, and it saved `stolen_A1.npy`. The recovered matrix is 20×10 and matches the real `A1` up to permuting and scaling rows: every true row has exactly one match, and the largest per-entry error is about 1.7e-8.

`steal.py` itself only calls `forward()`. To check the result, I ran a separate one-off script that compared it against the `A1` defined in `forward.py`. That check is not part of `steal.py`.

**How it works:**
1. **Find kinks:** it picks random lines through input space, and on each one finds the exact points where the output bends. Each bend is where one hidden neuron switches on or off.
2. **Get the row:** at each bend it estimates the gradient just before and just after. The difference is that neuron's row of `A1`, multiplied by its output weight in `A2`.
3. **Remove duplicates:** each row is normalised and its sign fixed. Rows are kept only if they turn up at least twice, and it stops after 15 lines in a row find no new neuron.

Each row of the output has length 1, and its sign may be flipped relative to the original. That fits "up to scaling" if negative scale factors count. One limitation: a neuron that never switches between −50 and 50 along the sampled lines, or whose output weight is near zero, can't be detected.
